Fall Golf in England:
Norfolk's Undiscovered Links Coast

July 2026  ·  7 min read  ·  By Golf East Anglia

← Back to Journal

Every fall, thousands of American golfers cross the Atlantic for the game's holy ground — St Andrews, Ballybunion, the Ayrshire coast — and almost all of them drive right past one of the best-kept secrets in the sport. On the far side of England, a couple of hours northeast of London, the Norfolk coast has been quietly playing genuine links and heathland golf since before most famous American clubs existed. Old Tom Morris pedigree. Clifftops over the North Sea. A fraction of the crowds, and a fraction of the cost.

If you already fly over for links golf, this is the trip you haven't taken yet — and fall is when to take it.

Where it is

Norfolk sits on England's east coast, northeast of London. You can fly into any of the London airports and drive up in around two and a half to three hours, or take the train to the city of Norwich and be on a first tee inside the hour. Norwich has its own airport, too. It slots neatly onto the end of a London trip — or onto a bigger UK-and-Ireland golf itinerary.

Why fall

Links and heathland golf is meant to be played on firm, running ground, and after the dry end of summer that's exactly what you get: fairways that chase, honest bounces, approach shots that ask you to think the ball onto the green rather than fly it there. The light goes long and golden, the summer visitors head home, tee times open up, and green fees ease off. More golf, fewer people, better value — that's the fall trade on this coast.

The courses — and the history

This isn't just pretty scenery. There's real history here, some of it your own:

Royal Cromer

Clifftop golf high above the North Sea, an Old Tom Morris original founded in 1888 and granted "Royal" status by the future King Edward VII. And here's the one that'll get you: it was on these cliffs that the idea for the Curtis Cup — the USA versus Great Britain & Ireland matches — was first hatched. You're playing on a piece of American golf history.

Sheringham

Runs right along the coast's edge, with a little steam railway puffing below the elevated holes and the sea filling every horizon.

Thetford

A complete change of pace just inland: classic Breckland heathland of ancient pines and heather, firm and tree-lined, and beautiful in October color.

Royal West Norfolk (Brancaster)

Timeless championship links out on the tidal marshes — wild, old-school, and the kind of round you'll be talking about back home.
