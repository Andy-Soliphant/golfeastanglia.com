Ryston Park Golf Club:
Nine Holes by James Braid

September 2026  ·  6 min read  ·  By Golf East Anglia

← Back to Journal

Most golfers heading for Norfolk turn north, towards the coast and the famous links. Almost nobody turns west. Which is how a James Braid course, laid out in 1932 and still following his routing, has stayed one of the quietest good rounds in the county.

Ryston Park sits at Denver, a mile or so south of Downham Market on the old Cambridge to King’s Lynn road. Nine holes, parkland, flat enough to walk without thinking about it, and greens that a lot of eighteen-hole clubs would be pleased to own.

The Braid connection

James Braid won five Opens and then spent the rest of his working life laying out golf courses — several hundred of them, Gleneagles among the best known. Norfolk has more of his work than most counties realise. His bunkering reshaped Hunstanton in 1907, and his hand is on courses at Thetford and across the Suffolk border too.

Ryston is the small one, and in some ways the purest. There has been no relocation, no rebuild, no lengthening to chase a modern driver. What you play is close to what he drew, on ground he chose because it drained.

The course

Nine holes played twice, and the second circuit is not the same golf as the first: different tees, different angles, and by then you know what the greens are doing. It measures 6,330 yards over the eighteen, which is a proper length for a course most people write off as a short one.

The trees do the defending. Braid used what was already there, and the mature timber that shapes several holes is a genuine obstacle in full leaf rather than decoration. The second is the hardest hole on the card — 436 yards, out of bounds and trees down the right, bunkers set to catch the safe side.

The one everyone talks about is the seventh, and it is the shortest hole on the course. A hundred and fifty-odd yards over water to a green with bunkers across the front and a tree leaning in from the left. Par there is worth having.

What it is like to be there

Quiet. That is the honest answer. This is west Norfolk, the flat country running down to the fens, and the course sits in old parkland with a church visible beyond the boundary across a sheep meadow. On a still afternoon you can go most of a round without seeing another group.

The clubhouse is a members’ clubhouse rather than a resort building, and it is the better for it. Food, a drink, and people who will talk to you about the course if you ask.

Where it fits in a Norfolk trip

Ryston is not a substitute for the coast, and we would not sell it as one. What it does is solve a real problem: the arrival day, the departure day, and the half-day nobody knows what to do with.

It sits close to the A10 and the Ely line, which makes it the natural first or last round for anyone coming up from Cambridge or London. Nine holes and a sandwich beats a wasted afternoon. It is also the answer when a group has mixed abilities — a flat nine is a far better day for an occasional golfer than thirty-six holes into a coastal wind.

And for anyone collecting Braid courses, it is a short detour to a complete and largely unaltered example of his work.

Playing it

Ryston is a members’ club and welcomes visitors and societies by arrangement rather than by online booking. That is a feature rather than an obstacle — a call gets you a tee time and usually a conversation. We handle that on behalf of groups, along with the rest of the trip.
