Nine nights.
Eight courses.
One day that belongs to nobody.

Long enough that the coast stops being a backdrop. Norfolk links, the Brecks inland, and two mornings on the Suffolk coast — with one day deliberately given to none of it.

Seven nights is a golf week. Nine is something else — long enough that the coast stops being a backdrop and starts being the reason you came.

The East Coast Week runs from London to Suffolk in seven nights and does it well, but it does it at a pace. Two extra nights change the arithmetic entirely: a day that belongs to nobody’s tee time, an inland movement through the Brecks that is currently a drive-through, and the Suffolk coast given two mornings rather than one.

It is also the version we would choose for a group where not everyone plays.

The stay

Four movements.

I

London

Night one

The night before

A transatlantic morning is a poor way to begin anything. The first night is in London with nothing asked of you, and dinner is booked if you want it and cancelled without comment if you do not.

North, in the morning

Under an hour to the clifftop at Cromer, and a short walk to the first tee. If the coast has other ideas — and across nine days here it will, once — the car is already waiting and the day runs as written.

II

The north coast

Nights two to six
Two

Royal Cromer, held for the morning

A clifftop course with the lighthouse in the middle of it and the sea on your shoulder for most of the round. We ask the club to close the sheet for the morning and put a starter on the first. Arranged at their discretion and ours, and never sold as certain until it is held.

Three

Sheringham

Ten minutes west and a different proposition entirely — a clifftop links where the railway runs below the fourth and the sea is in view from nearly every hole. Lunch after, rather than a hurried nine somewhere else.

Four

Royal West Norfolk, on the tide

Brancaster. The road across the marsh is under water twice a day and there is no second entrance, so this is the one morning of the nine that is not ours to schedule. Everything else is built outward from whatever window the tide gives us.

Five

The day that belongs to nobody

No golf. This is the day the seven-night version cannot afford and the reason to take nine.

What goes in it depends on the month and on the group. The seals at Blakeney, privately rather than on the shared boat. A skippered day through the creeks as they fill and empty. Holkham and the walled garden. Or, if the tides fall right, the wader spectacular at Snettisham — tens of thousands of birds lifting off the Wash together on the biggest tides of the year, which is not something that can be booked, only met.

Six

Hunstanton

Thirty minutes west, and the course a good many people who have played both quietly prefer. Dunes, hard ground, and a wind that decides the round. The last of the links before the ground changes underfoot.

III

The Brecks

Nights seven and eight
Seven

Thetford

Heathland through the forest, and Braid’s work. On a seven-night trip this is a course played on the way somewhere else; here it is played properly, from a bed twenty minutes away, with the afternoon still intact.

Eight

The afternoon in the Brecks

Two things sit within twenty minutes of the first tee, and neither is what anyone expects of Norfolk.

At Roudham, the first registered whisky distillery in England for over a century — local barley, water drawn from the aquifer beneath the still house, and a single malt named the best in the world at the 2024 World Whisky Awards. A private tasting there, after a round, tends to reorder people’s assumptions about the county.

And at Weeting, a ladder nine metres down into a Neolithic flint mine, dug four thousand years ago and the only one in Britain you can enter. Flint is what this entire coast is built from. This is where it came out of the ground.

IV

The Suffolk coast

Nights nine and ten
Nine

Royal Worlington & Newmarket

Nine holes inland that a great many good judges call the finest nine in the world. Played twice, as it is meant to be, and it takes less of the day than you expect — which is part of the point, and leaves the afternoon for the drive east to the coast.

Ten

Aldeburgh, then Thorpeness

Heathland on the Suffolk coast, and two courses within sight of each other that ask entirely different questions. Aldeburgh is the sterner of the two and the one people remember; Thorpeness is the one they enjoy.

Between or after them: Snape Maltings and Britten’s house, the shingle at Aldeburgh, and — for a group with an appetite for the genuinely strange — Orford Ness, reached only by the National Trust ferry, where the pagodas of a closed atomic weapons programme are still standing on the shingle.

Home

The car, when it is time

To the airport, or back to London. Groups who would rather not end on a travel day stay a further night on the coast, and we would not argue with them.

Why nine and not seven

Three things the shorter week cannot hold.

A day with no tee time in it

Seven nights of golf is seven mornings accounted for. The fifth day here belongs to the coast instead, and it is the day that makes the trip memorable rather than merely good.

The Brecks, properly

On the seven-night week Thetford is a course played on the way south. Here it has a movement of its own, and with it the whisky and the flint mines — two things nobody comes to England expecting and everybody talks about afterwards.

Two mornings on the Suffolk coast

Aldeburgh and Thorpeness within sight of each other, rather than one of them squeezed against a departure. It is also where the non-golfing half of a group is best served, between Snape, the shingle and Orford Ness.

And the season becomes an argument

Seals pup between November and January, samphire is cut in high summer, the shooting begins in September, and the waders answer to the tide table. Nine nights is long enough to build a stay around one of them — which makes it a stay that could not have happened at any other time of year.

What is arranged around it

The same as every commission.

Nothing on this page is an upgrade to be asked for. It is what a commission under By Arrangement includes, and the itinerary above is simply where it happens to go.

A car and driver, throughout

Not on call. There for the whole stay — to the courses, to dinner, and to Holkham with anyone who would rather not play. Clubs move separately, so nobody handles a bag between the first tee and the last green.

Dinner worth changing for

Morston Hall has held a Michelin star for twenty-five years and is fifteen minutes from where you are staying. Meadowsweet at Holt and The Neptune at Old Hunstanton are the other two on this coast. Tables are held before you arrive, because in season they are not there to be found on the night.

Caddies and a professional

Caddies arranged in advance where the club has them. A PGA professional with you throughout, for a group who would rather play better by the end than merely play.

For those not playing

Shooting, sailing on the marshes, the seals at Blakeney, a day at Holkham. Arranged to the same standard as the golf, and never as an afterthought — most groups here include somebody who has come for the coast rather than the courses.
