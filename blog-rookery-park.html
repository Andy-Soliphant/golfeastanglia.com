Rookery Park Golf Club:
Position Over Power

September 2026  ·  5 min read  ·  By Golf East Anglia

← Back to Journal

We played Rookery Park on 1 September, and it is a course we rate. It does not appear in the national rankings and does not need to. It is a well-kept members’ parkland course that asks you to think, and it looks after visitors properly.

The club sits on the Beccles road at Carlton Colville, on the edge of Lowestoft, in the corner of Suffolk where the county runs up against the Norfolk Broads. Eighteen holes of mature parkland, a separate nine-hole par-3 course, a floodlit driving range and a clubhouse with a bar and restaurant.

Rookery Park at a glance

How it plays

Length is not what Rookery Park asks of you. A good number of the holes are dog legs, and the trees on the corners are there to make you choose a line. You pick a line from the tee, put the ball on the right side of the fairway, and play the hole from there. Anyone used to reaching for the driver on every par four will find themselves thinking twice.

It isn’t overly long, but the dog legs mean you have to play golf, not just bomb drives.

There are four par fives and four par threes. The fifth, at around 550 yards, is the longest hole on the card. The ninth, at 138 yards, is the shortest, and it is well bunkered. The eighth is stroke index one.

The greens

On 1 September, at the end of a dry summer, the greens were in excellent condition. The club credits head green-keeper Neil Davey and his team, and it shows. For a visiting group, the greens are the first thing people talk about afterwards, and here they are worth talking about.

The welcome

Better than the greens, though, was the welcome. Rookery Park is a members’ club run by a board its members elect, and it behaves like one: visitors are treated as guests rather than as a green fee. For a group that has travelled some distance, that counts for more than anything on the scorecard.

A club older than its course

The club goes back to 1887, when a group of golfers began playing on the Denes at Lowestoft. It moved to Pakefield in 1906, and in 1924 James Braid laid out a full eighteen holes there. Housing and road schemes eventually made that site unworkable, the Rookery Estate was bought in 1972, and the present course opened in the late summer of 1975. The name comes from the farm the course was built on.

The designer, C. D. Lawrie, also advised the R&A’s championship committee. Fifty years on, the parkland has grown up around his holes. The Pakefield course is gone; if you want to play a Braid course that survives largely as he laid it out, see our guide to Ryston Park.

Being honest about where it sits

Rookery Park is not one of the headline courses of the Suffolk coast, and we would not sell it as one. It is a very good members’ parkland course, well kept and well run, and that is exactly what a golf trip needs on the days between the big rounds.

Where it fits in a trip

Its position in the north-east corner of Suffolk makes it useful in two directions. It is the natural parkland round for a group staying on the Broads or in the Waveney Valley, and it pairs well with Great Yarmouth & Caister, a short drive up the coast into Norfolk: links one day, parkland the next. For a Suffolk coast trip based around Southwold or Aldeburgh, it is the northern end of the week.

The par-3 course and the range also make it a good choice for a mixed group. The stronger players take on the main course while others play the short course or book a lesson with the club’s PGA professional.
